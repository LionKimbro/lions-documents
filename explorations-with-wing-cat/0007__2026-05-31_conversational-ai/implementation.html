Implementation

A solo-builder roadmap from microphone to social presence

The practical path is not training a giant model. It is orchestrating existing pieces: audio, transcription, diarization, voice recognition, event logs, memory, reasoning, queueing, and social arbitration.

Learning Areas

What must be learned

Real-time audio

Microphone capture, chunking, latency, sample rates, echo cancellation, device routing.

Transcription

Speech-to-text or realtime speech models, timestamps, confidence, transcript fidelity.

Diarization

Segment the stream by distinct voices: Speaker A spoke here, Speaker B overlapped there.

Voice recognition

Identify who is speaking through consent-based voice profiles, enrollment, and confidence scoring.

Turn-taking

Pause detection, direct address, speaker identity, interruption risk, backchannels, and repair moves.

LLM orchestration

Surface/deep split, structured events, tool use, private wonders, candidate musings.

Observability

A cockpit showing transcript, events, active wonders, standing score, and suppressed speech.

Technology Reality Check

The pieces exist; the social organism does not

As of 2026-05-31, most component technologies needed for this project already exist. The missing part is the integrated participation layer: the system that combines recognition, timing, memory, turn-taking, consent, and social judgment into one coherent room presence.

Capability Status Today Why It Matters
Speech-to-text Available and mature Streaming transcription can turn live speech into text; OpenAI's Realtime API also supports realtime audio transcription.
Word and segment timestamps Available Timing data is the raw material for pauses, overlap, interruptions, and turn-taking decisions.
Speaker diarization Available Toolkits and services can answer "who spoke when," usually with generic speaker labels at first.
Real-time diarization Partly available Microsoft Azure documents real-time diarization, but low-latency accuracy in noisy, overlapping rooms is still harder than offline analysis.
Known-speaker identification Available with enrollment Voiceprints can match audio against known speakers, but this should be explicit, consent-based, and confidence-scored.
Realtime speech-to-speech conversation Available OpenAI's Realtime API supports low-latency speech-to-speech interaction with multimodal inputs and outputs.
Slang, prosody, intonation, emotion Partly available Audio-native models can preserve or respond to more nuance than text-only pipelines, but fine-grained social interpretation remains uneven.
Human-grade interruption judgment Partly available APIs can handle interruptions mechanically; deciding when interruption is socially right is still an orchestration problem.
Full room-aware social presence Not off the shelf The system described in this report still requires custom integration: social governor, musing queue, speaker awareness, memory boundaries, and modes.

The key distinction is recognition versus participation. Recognition can say "Speaker 1 said these words." Participation needs to infer "Lion is talking to Robert, not me; this is banter; I have a useful thought, but I should wait."

Phases

The build ladder

Phase 0

Define the first tiny target

Mic to VAD to transcription to LLM to text response. No diarization, no social subtlety.

About 1 week

The first version should only prove the basic signal path: capture sound from the microphone, detect when speech is happening, turn the spoken audio into text, send that text to a language model, and display a short reply. It will not yet know whether Lion or Robert is speaking, and it will not know whether it is socially appropriate to answer. This phase is deliberately crude: it confirms that the audio plumbing, transcript, model call, and output loop all work.

Phase 1

Live transcript machine

Show speech started, transcript segment, speech ended, and save events to JSONL.

2-4 weeks

This phase turns the rough demo into an observable instrument. The system should show when speech begins, what text was recognized, when speech ends, and what timing metadata belongs to each segment. The saved event log becomes the black box recorder for debugging everything that follows.

Phase 2

Address detection

Notice names, questions, long pauses, and whether the AI was likely addressed.

2-4 weeks

The goal here is restraint. The system starts learning the difference between nearby speech and speech directed at it. Simple cues such as names, question forms, explicit requests, and pauses after questions let the agent answer less often but more appropriately.

Phase 3

Surface and deep mind split

Create private wonder packets and let the deep mind return musings instead of direct speech.

1-2 months

This is where the architecture stops being a simple voice chatbot. The fast surface layer can raise private questions while the slower reasoning layer works in the background. The important shift is that thinking and speaking become separate operations.

Phase 4

Musing queue and social governor

Score importance, urgency, standing, social risk, and decide speak/wait/drop/compress.

2-4 months

Candidate thoughts now become managed objects. A useful idea can wait, shrink, expire, or be suppressed if the conversational moment is wrong. This phase proves that the agent can have something to say without automatically saying it.

Phase 5

Voice-aware speaker tracking

Add diarization first, then consent-based voice recognition so the system can distinguish distinct voices and map known voices to people.

3-9 months

The system begins to understand that a room has multiple human participants. First it separates voices into Speaker A, Speaker B, and so on; later it can map enrolled voices to names with confidence scores. This is what lets it distinguish “Lion is talking to me” from “Lion is talking to Robert.”

Phase 6

Backchannels and interruption

Add restrained social acts such as "I am with you" or "I have a thought when there is room."

2-6 months

Participation becomes more nuanced than full answers. The system can acknowledge, briefly repair, ask for a repeat, or signal that it has a thought waiting. This phase is delicate: small social moves should make the agent feel present, not noisy.

Risks

Complexity traps to avoid

Separate the voice problems. Transcription finds words; diarization separates speakers; voice recognition identifies known people.

Do not start with automatic identity recognition. Begin with Speaker A/B, manual labels, and explicit voice enrollment.

Do not train models first. The invention is the coordination architecture.

Make the first version too quiet. Polite silence beats brilliant interruption.

Build observability early. You need to see why it spoke, waited, or suppressed a thought.

Evaluate social behavior, not just accuracy. Track false interruptions, missed invitations, awkward latency, and unwanted memory use.

Design mode boundaries. Quiet, companion, meeting, teaching, and brainstorming modes should change speaking rights explicitly.
